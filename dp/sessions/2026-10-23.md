On the vocabulary — and you're closer than you think:

- **Fiber** is right for *the set behind a state*. You need a map first: partial solutions → states. Each state's class is the preimage of a point, which is precisely a fiber. So "the fiber over state `s`" is correct and standard.
- The *relation* itself has a proper name: **Nerode equivalence**. Two prefixes are Nerode-equivalent when no suffix distinguishes them — that is word-for-word "no future can tell them apart." And here's the kicker: the **Myhill–Nerode theorem** says the states of the *minimal* automaton are exactly the Nerode classes. Your DP state graph **is** an automaton — cells are states, moves are letters, "reaching `(H-1,W-1)`" is acceptance — so DP state design is DFA minimisation under a different hat. Every time you collapse `(i,j,last-move)` down to the fewest slots that still work, you're minimising an automaton.
- The structure is a **quotient**: states are the quotient set of partial solutions by that relation, and the map to states is the quotient map. For the quotient to be legal at all, the relation has to be a **congruence** — compatible with the transitions, which is exactly "the transition treats the whole fiber uniformly." That's your point, stated algebraically.
- The cousin you'll also see: **bisimulation** (each state simulates the other's moves — coinductive, common in concurrency and RL), and **state abstraction** / **sufficient statistic** in MDP language, where the Markov property is literally "the state is a sufficient statistic of the history for the future."

One crack worth knowing now, because it's where the clean picture bends: **counting is an equivalence problem, optimisation is a dominance problem.** For counting, all your fibres must be *identical* in their suffix sets. For min/max, two prefixes at the same cell with the same legal suffixes can still differ — they arrive with different accumulated cost — and the resolution isn't merging them, it's discarding the dominated one. `min` is not an equivalence quotient, it's a proof that one member can never win. So the "no future can tell them apart" test works verbatim for counting, and needs the weaker form "no future prefers one" for optimisation. Both are the same instinct: throw away what the future cannot use.

And "partial solution" is fine — the literature says *prefix*, *partial solution*, or *representative of the class* interchangeably.

Mostly yes — one thing is off by a direction, and it's worth fixing because it's the only piece that's actually a *definition* rather than a picture. Going clause by clause.

**"fiber is some churner or funnel which takes partial solutions into a single state"** — the picture is right, the grammar is backwards. The **map** is the thing that takes a partial solution to a state. The **fiber** is what's *sitting inside* the bucket once it lands — the collection of everything that got sent there. So:

$$\sigma:\ \text{partial solution} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{state},\qquad \text{fiber}(s) = \sigma^{-1}(s)$$

Fiber is a *set*, not a function. It's the answer to "which partial solutions live here," never "which state does this go to." The funnel is `σ`; the pile at the bottom is the fiber.

**"partial solution is a set of choices made so far"** — sequences, not sets, and they carry a number. The order has to survive, and here's the concrete proof using our own grid. Under the no-two-rights rule, all of these reach `(2,3)` and all of them are the *same multiset* `{R,R,R,D,D}`:

```
RDRDR   legal     ─┐ same multiset,
DRRDR   illegal   ─┘ different futures
```

Identical as sets, different as walks, and the rule can tell them apart. So a partial solution is `RDRDR` — an ordered sequence — plus whatever it has accumulated (in Panel 3, the `3` or the `9`). Two components: the choices, in order, and the running value.

**"to reach this particular state"** — drop that clause. It makes the definition circular, because a partial solution doesn't know what state it belongs to; that's the map's job, not its own. A partial solution is just a walk. The state is a verdict passed on it afterwards.

**"state is a view / squint under which all those partial solutions look the same"** — that's the best phrasing anyone's put on it in this conversation, and I'd keep it. One sharpening only: the squint isn't chosen because *we* stop caring about the differences. It's forced by **what the future asks**. You squint exactly as hard as the remaining questions allow and not one degree harder — squint past that and you've crammed two different futures into one box.

**"given equivalence or domination, reduction happens to a value"** — yes, and the two rules are different reductions:

| mechanism | what the fiber does | stored value |
|---|---|---|
| equivalence (counting) | all members are kept | sum |
| domination (min/max) | all but the best are killed | min / max |

Same fiber, two different fates. That's the whole of Panels 1 and 3.

Now the concrete fibers, on the grid you already own, no constraint:

| state `s` | fiber `σ⁻¹(s)` — the actual walks | value |
|---|---|---|
| `(1,1)` | `RD`, `DR` | `2` |
| `(2,1)` | `DDR`, `DRD`, `RDD` | `3` |
| `(2,3)` | all 10 interleavings of `RRR` and `DD` | `10` |

and with the no-two-rights rule, `(2,3)` splits into:

| state `s` | fiber | value |
|---|---|---|
| `(2,3)` arrived by `D` | *(empty)* | `0` |
| `(2,3)` arrived by `R` | `{RDRDR}` | `1` |

An empty fiber is a perfectly normal thing — it's a state whose question has the answer zero. That's your wall, once more.

So the compact version to hold onto:

> **The state key is the map. The table is its fibers. A wrong answer is a fiber with two different futures crammed inside it, and a waste of memory is a fiber with only one member.**

That last sentence is the whole of state design: coarsen until you'd merge two different futures, refine until every fiber earns its keep.

Your first sentence is a definition, not an approximation: **fiber = preimage of a state, i.e. who contributed behind the scenes.** That's it, exactly.

**On "why do we say the state-key is the map"** — you've caught my shorthand and it was sloppy. `σ` is the function; a state-key is a *value* in `σ`'s output. What I meant is narrower and more useful: **choosing which fields go into the key is what defines `σ`.** If the key is `(i, j)` then `σ(x) = ` "the cell `x` ends on." If the key is `(i, j, last-move)` then `σ` is a different function. You don't write `σ` down anywhere; you write the field list, and `σ` is whatever function reads exactly those fields. So the key's *fields* specify the map; a key's *value* is `σ`'s output. My sentence should have been "the state key's definition is the map."

**On the union** — yes, with one upgrade: it's not merely a union, it's a **partition**. The fibers are disjoint (a walk lands in exactly one cell) and together they cover everything. That's why `σ` is a quotient rather than a covering — every partial solution belongs to one and only one fiber, no double-filing, no orphans. Your Grid 1 bug with `right[0][0] = down[0][0] = 1` was precisely a violation of this: one partial solution (the empty walk) double-filed into two fibers, and the count came out `2` where `1` was true.

**On thick fibers** — right instinct, one word to add. Thicker is not *better*, thicker is *cheaper*, and it's free only up to the point where the future stops being able to tell members apart. So the rule is **thickest legal**:

> Too thin → one fiber per walk, and you've built a brute force.
> Too thick → two different futures sharing a fiber, and the number is wrong.
> Just thick enough → every fiber answers every remaining question identically.

Your algorithmic frame is exactly right, and here's what makes it land numerically. Our own problem, `H = W = 1000`, plain Grid 1:

- **Domain** (all partial solutions): every monotone walk from `(0,0)` to anywhere. The full trips alone number $\binom{1998}{999} \approx 10^{600}$.
- **Fiber count**: $10^6$ cells.
- **Thickness**: each fiber clubs roughly $10^{594}$ concrete walks into one integer.

So the table doesn't store $10^{600}$ walks. It stores $10^6$ numbers, each of which is a *count* standing in for a galaxy of walks the future can't distinguish. That ratio — $10^{600}$ down to $10^6$ — **is** dynamic programming. And the no-two-rights rule cost you a factor of two: same domain, fibers split by last move, $2 \cdot 10^6$ cells. One bit of key, one doubling of the table. That's the exchange rate you're always trading at.

The mental model I'd keep, since you're right that a text-in-boxes diagram adds nothing here:

> **Postal sorting.** Every walk is an envelope. `σ` is the ZIP code written on it. Every envelope with the same ZIP goes in the same bin — the bin is the fiber. The depot needs one bin per ZIP, not one slot per envelope, which is why the whole mail system fits in a warehouse.
>
> And the two failure modes are physical. Sort by **country** only and your bins are enormous and useless — the driver can't deliver, because envelopes bound for different streets got mixed. Sort by **house** and you have one envelope per bin, a warehouse the size of the planet. The state key is you deciding, in advance, how fine the sorting has to be for the rest of the route to work.

Notice what the bins *don't* do: they never lose an envelope. Nothing is thrown away in the coarse case either — the count is still exhaustive. What breaks is the *delivery*, because the bin's single number now has to answer a question that its contents disagree about.

Which is the same sentence as your own conclusion, just with the failure mode attached: **thick enough to be cheap, thin enough that every member of the bin gives the same answer to every question still to come.**
